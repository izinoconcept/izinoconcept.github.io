How to Choose a Hospital for a Corporate Retainership: A Due-Diligence Checklist

By the i.zino concept team · Published 28 October 2026 · 7 min read

Choosing the hospital is the most consequential decision in a medical retainership. The best-drafted contract cannot make up for a facility that cannot cope with your staff's needs at 2 a.m. This checklist is the kind of due diligence we encourage companies to carry out before we facilitate an agreement, and it works just as well if you are doing the search yourself.

Step 1: Define what you need before you look

Write down the realistic demands: the number of employees and dependants, where they live and work, the most likely conditions (general outpatient care, maternity, injuries, occupational health), and any special requirements such as pre-employment or periodic medicals. Without this, you will compare hospitals on impressions rather than fit.

Step 2: Check the basics

  • Registration and licensing. Ask for evidence that the facility is registered and licensed to operate by the relevant state health authority, and that its doctors and nurses hold current registration with their professional councils. Ask to see, not just be told.
  • Ownership and standing. Confirm the registered name of the business, who runs it, and how long it has operated.
  • Insurance. Ask whether it carries professional indemnity cover.

Step 3: Inspect the facility in person

A visit tells you more than a brochure. Look at the following, and take notes:

  • Emergency capacity. Is there genuine round-the-clock cover with a doctor on site or on rapid call? How many beds are available for observation and admission? Is there an ambulance, and who staffs it?
  • Diagnostics. Are laboratory and basic imaging done in-house, and how quickly are results returned? Outsourced tests add delay.
  • Specialists. Which specialties are available full-time, which are visiting, and on what days? For example, obstetrics and gynaecology, paediatrics, general surgery, orthopaedics, and ophthalmology.
  • Pharmacy. Is it stocked for common prescriptions, so that patients are not sent out to buy drugs?
  • Power and water. What is the backup if the grid fails? A hospital without reliable backup power cannot guarantee service.
  • Hygiene and infection control. General cleanliness, handling of waste, and separation of patient areas are visible within minutes.
  • Patient flow. How are registration, waiting and triage handled? Would a corporate patient be seen promptly?
  • Records. Are patient records organised and kept confidentially?

Step 4: Test the corporate side of the business

Many hospitals are strong clinically but have never managed a corporate client. Ask:

  • Is there a named corporate-services or account officer?
  • Can they produce itemised invoices that match authorisations?
  • How do they handle pre-authorisation, and referrals to tertiary facilities?
  • Can they provide periodic utilisation reports?
  • What is their position on payment terms?

Step 5: Take references

Ask for the names of other corporate clients and contact two or three. Ask them about response times, billing accuracy and how problems were resolved. Be sceptical of a hospital that cannot or will not provide any.

Step 6: Score and compare

Put the findings into a simple matrix so the decision is transparent and can be explained to management. Weight the criteria according to your needs, for instance emergency capacity higher for a field workforce, maternity and paediatrics higher for a family-heavy staff.

Multiply each score by its weight and add the totals. The highest total is a starting point for discussion, not an automatic winner.

Step 7: Start with a pilot or a review period

Where possible, agree a shorter initial term or a formal review after the first three to six months. Real usage will show whether waiting times, billing and communication match what was promised, and the contract should allow both sides to adjust.

A note for hospitals

If you run a hospital or clinic and want corporate retainerships, the same checklist is your preparation. Have your licences ready, designate a corporate account officer, prepare a clear tariff and a sample utilisation report, and be honest about the capacity you can commit. A smaller commitment kept reliably wins more renewals than a large one that is missed. This is the kind of preparation that makes our introductions between companies and hospitals go smoothly, and you are welcome to get in touch if you want to discuss it.

This checklist is general guidance and not a substitute for professional medical, legal or regulatory advice.
