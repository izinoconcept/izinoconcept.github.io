Pre-Employment and Periodic Medical Examinations: A Practical Guide for Employers

By the i.zino concept team · Published 8 November 2026 · 6 min read

Many employers, especially in oil and gas, construction and other physically demanding sectors, require staff to pass a medical examination before starting work and at intervals afterwards. Done well, these examinations protect workers, help the employer place people in suitable roles, and give an early warning of health problems. Done badly, they become a paperwork exercise, or worse, a source of disputes about privacy and fairness. This guide sets out what to decide before you commission them.

Start with the purpose

An examination should answer a specific question: is this person fit to do this job safely? That is different from a full diagnostic check-up. Being clear about the purpose keeps the scope reasonable, avoids collecting information you do not need, and makes the outcome easier to act on.

  • Pre-employment (or pre-placement) examinations establish a baseline and confirm fitness for the role before the person starts.
  • Periodic examinations monitor health at agreed intervals, especially where the work involves particular exposures or physical demands.
  • Return-to-work examinations follow a long illness or injury.
  • Special assessments apply before assignments with unusual demands, such as remote or offshore work, where access to care is limited.

Match the content to the job's risks

There is no single standard package. A sensible examination is built from the job's real hazards, and the examining doctor has the final say on what is clinically appropriate. Common elements include:

  • Medical history and a general physical examination
  • Vision and hearing tests, particularly for drivers, machine operators and noisy environments
  • Blood pressure, weight and basic laboratory tests
  • Chest imaging or lung-function tests where dust, fumes or chemicals are involved
  • Specific checks linked to the role, such as the physical capacity for heavy lifting or working at height

Avoid adding tests simply because they are available. Every extra test adds cost and collects more personal information.

Define the possible outcomes

The report the employer receives should be a clear fitness conclusion, not a medical file. A typical set of categories is:

OutcomeMeaning
FitNo health reason to prevent the person doing the job.
Fit with restrictionsCan do the job with stated adjustments, for example no work at height or limited lifting.
Temporarily unfitShould not start or continue until a condition is treated or reviewed.
UnfitThe condition makes the job unsafe for the person or others, even with adjustments.

Agree in advance what the company will do for each outcome, including how an employee can ask for a second opinion. Decisions based on a medical result should be fair, consistent and documented, and employers should take legal advice before acting on an adverse result.

Protect confidentiality

Medical information is among the most sensitive personal data. The employer usually needs the fitness conclusion and any workplace restrictions, not the diagnosis. The doctor should explain this to the employee before the examination, and the employee should know who will see the report. Records should be stored securely by the hospital, separately from the personnel file, and handled in line with Nigeria's data protection law.

Set a sensible frequency

How often to repeat an examination depends on the risk. Roles with significant exposures or physical demands may need annual checks, while lower-risk office roles may need far less. Age, existing conditions and regulatory or client requirements can all affect the schedule. Ask the examining doctor to advise, then write the schedule into your policy so everyone is treated alike.

Plan the logistics

  • Booking and turnaround. Agree how appointments are made, how long reports take, and how urgent cases are handled.
  • Volume. Large intakes or shutdown periods put pressure on a hospital, so give notice.
  • Cost. Decide who pays and whether examinations are priced per package or per test.
  • Standard forms. A standard report template makes results comparable and easy to file.

Put it in the retainership

If you have a medical retainership with a hospital, the examination programme should be a named schedule in it: the packages, prices, turnaround times, report format and confidentiality rules. Our guide to what a retainership agreement should cover shows where it fits, and our checklist for choosing a hospital lists what to look for in diagnostics and staffing before you commit.

Common mistakes

  • Requiring the same extensive package for every role, regardless of risk
  • Letting the employer see detailed diagnoses
  • Having no process for staff who receive an adverse result
  • Failing to keep records that show the programme was applied consistently

General information only. It is not medical or legal advice. Examination content and employment decisions should be guided by qualified doctors and your legal adviser.
