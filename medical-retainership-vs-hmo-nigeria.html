Medical Retainership or HMO? How Nigerian Companies Can Decide

By the i.zino concept team · Published 28 October 2026 · 6 min read

When a company decides to look after its employees' healthcare, two routes come up again and again. The first is to buy cover through a Health Maintenance Organisation (HMO), which contracts a network of hospitals and manages claims. The second is to contract one or more hospitals directly under a medical retainership. Neither is better in every situation. The right answer depends on where your people work, how many there are and how much control you want.

How each model works

HMO cover. The employer pays the HMO a premium, usually per person. Staff then use hospitals in the HMO's network, and the HMO handles authorisations and pays the hospitals. The HMO carries part of the financial risk of high usage, in return for the premium.

Medical retainership. The employer agrees terms directly with a chosen hospital or small group of hospitals: services, prices, response times and payment. The employer carries the usage risk (unless the contract is a capped or capitated arrangement) but also gets more say in how the service is delivered. Our guide to what a retainership agreement should cover sets out the clauses involved.

Questions to ask before choosing

  1. Where do our people actually work and live? A team based around one city can be served well by a local hospital arrangement. A workforce spread across the country needs reach.
  2. How much do we need to control response times? Companies with shift work, remote sites or safety-critical roles often want guaranteed emergency access and fast-tracked medicals, which are easier to negotiate directly.
  3. How stable is our headcount? Premium-based cover scales per head. A direct arrangement works best when staff numbers and usage are reasonably predictable.
  4. Do we have the capacity to manage it? Someone has to review invoices, track utilisation and hold review meetings. If you have no one for this, either choose the HMO route or use an adviser.
  5. What have staff complained about before? Long waiting times, refused authorisations and limited choice point to different answers.

The hybrid option

These are not mutually exclusive. Some employers keep an HMO plan for general cover across the country and add a retainership with a hospital near their main base for emergencies, occupational health examinations and priority access. If you go this way, define clearly which arrangement pays for what, so that bills do not fall between the two.

What to check on either route

  • That the provider, whether HMO or hospital, is properly licensed and in good standing with the relevant regulator.
  • How complaints and disputes are escalated, and how fast.
  • What happens to a patient in the middle of treatment if the arrangement ends.
  • How staff data is protected and who can see it.

Our view

We do not push one model. Our work is introducing companies, particularly in oil and gas, to hospitals for retainership arrangements, so we naturally see the cases where a direct relationship adds value: concentrated workforces, strong occupational-health needs and a wish for named contacts. But where a workforce is dispersed, or a company wants minimal administration, an HMO may well be the better fit. If you are weighing the two, talk to us and we will walk through your situation honestly, including when a retainership is not the right answer.
