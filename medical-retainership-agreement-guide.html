What a Medical Retainership Agreement Should Cover: A Clause-by-Clause Guide

By the i.zino concept team · Published 28 October 2026 · 7 min read

A medical retainership agreement is a contract under which a company, often in oil and gas or another large employer, designates a hospital or clinic to look after its staff's healthcare needs on agreed terms. For the company it brings predictable access and cost. For the hospital it brings a steady flow of patients and a clearer view of income. Both benefits depend on how well the agreement is written.

Disputes in this area rarely come from bad faith. They usually come from vague wording: nobody wrote down who is covered, how fast the hospital must respond, or when an invoice is due. The clauses below are the ones worth settling in writing before the first patient walks in.

1. Parties and who is covered

Name the company and the hospital precisely, using the registered names. Then define the covered population: permanent staff only, or also contract staff, dependants and next of kin? If dependants are included, say how many per employee and whether spouses and children are treated the same way. Agree how the company will notify the hospital of new joiners and leavers, because an out-of-date staff list is the most common source of billing disagreements.

2. Scope of services

List what is included and what is not. Typical headings are outpatient consultations, laboratory and imaging, pharmacy, inpatient care, surgery, maternity, dental and eye care, and emergencies. Be equally clear about exclusions, such as cosmetic procedures or treatment abroad, and about services the hospital cannot provide itself and will refer onward. Where the company's work involves pre-employment or periodic medical examinations, or fitness checks before field or offshore assignments, put those in the scope explicitly, with the format of the resulting report.

3. Pricing model

There are three common ways to price a retainership, and many agreements combine them:

  • Flat retainer. The company pays a fixed monthly or annual sum for a defined bundle of services. It is easy to budget but needs a clear limit on what the bundle covers.
  • Fee-for-service at an agreed tariff. The company pays for each service used, at a price list attached to the contract. It is flexible, but costs vary with usage.
  • Per-head (capitation). The company pays a set amount per covered person. It transfers some utilisation risk to the hospital, so the hospital will want realistic assumptions and sometimes a cap on high-cost cases.

Whichever model is chosen, attach the tariff as a schedule and state how and when it can be reviewed. Silent price increases are a quick way to damage a relationship.

4. Service levels and response times

This is the clause that turns a hospital listing into a working partnership. Set measurable expectations: emergency access around the clock, maximum waiting time for scheduled appointments, turnaround times for laboratory results, and how corporate patients are prioritised or fast-tracked. If the company's staff work at remote sites, agree how ambulance or transfer support is arranged and who pays for it.

5. Referrals and authorisations

No private hospital can do everything. Agree how referrals to specialists or tertiary facilities are handled, who authorises high-cost procedures, and what the hospital must do in an emergency when pre-approval is not possible. A short, named list of contacts on each side (usually an operations or HR lead for the company and a corporate-services officer for the hospital) saves many hours.

6. Billing and payment

State the invoicing cycle, what must accompany each invoice (for example, patient identification and a service breakdown), the payment period, and how disputed items are handled so that a disagreement over one line does not freeze payment of the rest. Late payment is a real strain on a private hospital's cash flow, so a clear payment period, and a sensible remedy if it is missed, protects both sides.

7. Confidentiality and records

Health information is sensitive personal data. The agreement should say who may see what: the company should normally receive fitness-for-work conclusions and utilisation summaries, not clinical detail about individual employees, unless the employee has consented. Both sides should also understand their obligations under Nigeria's data protection law and handle records accordingly.

8. Reporting and review

A short monthly or quarterly utilisation report (visits, common conditions in aggregate, turnaround performance, complaints) lets the company check value for money and lets the hospital plan capacity. Build in a scheduled review meeting, at least annually, where pricing and service levels can be adjusted by agreement.

9. Professional standards, liability and insurance

The hospital should confirm that it and its practitioners hold the registrations and licences required to practise, and that it carries appropriate professional indemnity cover. Clinical decisions remain the hospital's responsibility; the company's obligations are payment and information. Say so plainly.

10. Term, renewal and termination

Set the start date, the length of the term and how renewal works. Include a notice period for termination by either side, and a transition clause so that patients in the middle of treatment are not left without care if the relationship ends. Finally, name the governing law and a practical way to resolve disputes, such as escalation to senior management followed by mediation or arbitration, before anyone considers court.

What the hospital gains, and why it should push for detail

For a private hospital, a corporate retainership offers predictable patient volume and a clearer cash-flow picture, and it lets the facility standardise how a particular client's staff are seen. That only works if the hospital can staff and equip itself for the load it has agreed to. Hospitals should therefore resist open-ended promises on response times or caseload and ask for realistic projections of staff numbers and expected usage before signing.

A note on drafting

The structure above is a checklist, not a template. Every arrangement has its own commercial reality, and the final contract should be reviewed by a lawyer qualified in Nigeria.
